# Section 2: What Is the Electrical Property?

The term "electrical property" often evokes concepts such as conductivity or electric charge, but in this context, it refers to a more fundamental and pervasive nature: the way particles and matter interact with space, other particles, and fields through their charge distribution and polarization.

Unlike the gravitational model which centers on mass and curvature of space-time, the electrical property model posits that all interactions—attraction, repulsion, motion, and spatial influence—can be traced to electric interactions. This includes not only visible currents and charges, but the latent electric fields resulting from the configuration of electrons within atoms and molecules.

Matter exhibits electrical characteristics by virtue of the structure and distribution of its electrons. Every atom is composed of electrons orbiting a nucleus, and these electrons are inherently carriers of negative charge. The aggregate behavior of these charges, whether aligned or neutralized, defines the macroscopic electrical property of the substance.

For example, a conductive metal allows electrons to move freely, hence responds readily to electric stimuli. A non-conductor or insulator like rubber restricts this movement, but still contains charged particles in fixed positions—thus contributing to its dielectric properties and influencing its interaction with other objects.

Electricity is not merely a current in wires; it is a universal descriptor of material behavior. When we say "electrical property," we are referring to the interactional capacity of a substance based on its electron structure. This, under the new hypothesis, is what generates gravitational behavior.

> Note: Even materials like insulators (e.g., rubber) that do not conduct electricity still possess structured electrons and influence spatial electric potential. Therefore, they can exhibit "gravitational properties." This will be examined in detail in the next section.
